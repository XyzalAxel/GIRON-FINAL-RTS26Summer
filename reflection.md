This project provided a practical opportunity to apply many of the real-time systems concepts covered throughout the course, including interrupt handling, FreeRTOS task scheduling, synchronization primitives, and timing analysis. While the overall application was relatively small, designing a system that behaved consistently under different workloads required much more planning and debugging than I initially expected.

One of the biggest challenges was obtaining meaningful latency measurements. At the beginning of the project, I assumed measuring interrupt-to-task response would simply involve recording two timestamps. As development progressed, I realized that many factors influence the measured latency, including task priorities, processor load, scheduling decisions, and even the overhead introduced by serial output. Producing measurements that were both repeatable and representative of system behavior required multiple iterations of testing and careful consideration of what was actually being measured.

Another challenge was designing the interrupt service routine correctly. Throughout the project, I learned that an ISR should perform only the minimum amount of work necessary before returning control to the scheduler. Initially it was tempting to place additional functionality inside the interrupt handler, but keeping the ISR limited to timestamping, signaling the waiting tasks, and generating the timing pulse resulted in a cleaner and more predictable design. Moving the remaining work into the bottom-half tasks better reflects how production embedded systems are typically implemented.

If I were to continue developing this project, I would separate the direct task notification and binary semaphore experiments into independent test configurations. In the current implementation, both bottom-half tasks are awakened by the same interrupt, meaning one task may influence the observed latency of the other because only one task can execute first. Testing each synchronization mechanism independently would provide a cleaner comparison of their performance. I would also validate the design on physical ESP32 hardware using an oscilloscope or external logic analyzer instead of relying solely on simulation.

The induced-failure experiment was one of the most valuable parts of the project. Removing the call to portYIELD_FROM_ISR() demonstrated that a system can continue functioning while exhibiting degraded real-time behavior. The project reinforced that many real-time software defects are not complete failures; instead, they appear as increased latency, scheduling delays, or reduced timing predictability. Understanding this distinction helped connect the theoretical scheduling concepts from class with practical embedded software development.

Overall, the most valuable lesson I learned is that designing a real-time system involves much more than writing functional code. Correct synchronization, careful interrupt design, task prioritization, and accurate measurement are all necessary to produce predictable behavior. This project strengthened my understanding of FreeRTOS while also demonstrating how engineering decisions affect system timing and reliability. The experience provided a much better appreciation for the challenges involved in developing embedded software for industrial, automotive, aerospace, and other safety-critical applications.
